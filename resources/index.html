Legal & safe-practice guidelines

Every module on this hub links back here. Read it once, fully, before you run a single offensive tool — and revisit it any time you're unsure whether something is in bounds.

This is not legal adviceLaws around computer access vary by country, state, and even by specific statute. Nothing here is a substitute for consulting an actual lawyer if you have a real doubt about a specific situation. When in doubt, don't run the tool.

~/legal/the-law.md

The legal baseline

  • In the United States, the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (18 U.S.C. § 1030) makes accessing a computer system "without authorization" a federal crime
  • Most other countries — the UK's Computer Misuse Act, the EU's Directive 2013/40/EU, and similar statutes elsewhere — carry equivalent prohibitions
  • Unauthorized access alone can be a crime, even with no data stolen and no damage caused
  • "I was just testing" or "I meant no harm" is not a legal defense to unauthorized access
~/legal/what-counts-as-authorized.md

What "authorized" actually means

  • Systems and VMs you personally own, on a network isolated from anyone else
  • Purpose-built practice targets: DVWA, Metasploitable, OWASP Juice Shop, VulnHub and HackTheBox machines
  • A client's or employer's systems, only under a signed contract defining scope, dates, and rules of engagement
  • Bug bounty programs — strictly within the exact scope the program publishes, nothing adjacent
~/legal/lab-hygiene.md

Lab hygiene

  • Keep attacker and target VMs on an isolated Host-only or Internal Network — never Bridged — during practice
  • Snapshot before every project so mistakes cost seconds, not hours
  • Never point a scan, exploit, or wireless attack tool at an IP or SSID you haven't personally confirmed you own or are authorized to test
  • Keep tools and VM images updated — an out-of-date lab can behave unpredictably in ways that are hard to debug
~/legal/if-you-find-something.md

If you stumble onto something real

  • Stop immediately — don't explore further or try to "confirm" the issue beyond what you already saw
  • Don't access, download, or modify any data beyond what you accidentally observed
  • Check whether the organization has a responsible disclosure policy or bug bounty program and report through it
  • If there's no clear reporting channel, document what you saw and consider contacting the organization's security team directly, without probing further

The simplest ruleIf you can't point to written permission or personal ownership of the system in front of you, don't run the tool. Every project on this hub is designed so you never have to make that judgment call.
